The Cold War's Strangest Mystery: How a Fish Fooled an Entire Navy

By WeirdHub Team • 20 September, 2026

For Ten Years, Sweden Was Hunting Ghosts Beneath the Waves

In the early 1980s, at the height of the Cold War, something strange began happening along the Swedish coastline. Deep beneath the surface, naval hydrophones kept picking up a mysterious, repetitive sound near secret military bases and quiet harbor waters. To the Swedish Navy, still shaken after a Soviet submarine ran aground near the Karlskrona naval base in 1981, this could only mean one thing: hostile submarines were secretly slipping into their territory. Officials feared the worst, ranging from ongoing surveillance to a possible act of war. Over the following years, the military responded with everything it had, deploying warships, dropping depth charges, and mobilizing helicopters to hunt down these silent intruders again and again. Yet no wreckage was ever found. No submarine was ever caught. No enemy ever surfaced. The strange underwater noise simply kept returning, night after night, year after year, as if something invisible was toying with an entire nation's defense system.

When Even Yeltsin Said, I Have No Idea What You Are Talking About

What made the mystery even stranger was that it refused to end. The Cold War officially wound down between 1989 and 1991, the Soviet Union collapsed, and tensions across Europe eased. Yet the same eerie sound kept showing up in Swedish waters, as active and persistent as ever. By 1994, the confusion had reached the highest levels of government. Sweden's own Prime Minister, Carl Bildt, wrote directly to Russian President Boris Yeltsin, asking him to explain the repeated underwater incursions and to put an end to them. Yeltsin's reply was essentially a shrug, insisting Russia had no idea what Sweden was referring to. With diplomacy leading nowhere and military patrols producing nothing but empty water, the Navy finally admitted it needed outside help. In 1996, for the first time, this classified acoustic data was handed over not to another admiral or intelligence officer, but to two civilian scientists, a marine biologist and a fisheries researcher, who were simply asked to listen.

A Fish Market Visit Solved What the Navy Could Not

What the researchers, Magnus Wahlberg and Hakan Westerberg, heard on those recordings did not sound like a warship at all. It sounded, as Wahlberg later described it, like bacon frying, a light, crackling stream of tiny air bubbles rising through water. Curious and increasingly convinced this was biological rather than mechanical, Wahlberg picked up a fresh herring from a local fish market and gently squeezed it. To everyone's astonishment, the fish produced the exact same crackling sound the Navy had been recording for over a decade. Further study revealed the surprising biology behind it, herring release excess gas from their swim bladder through their rear end, creating tiny, rapid bursts of sound that travel remarkably well underwater. When massive schools of herring move together, sometimes stretching for kilometers, the combined noise can closely mimic the acoustic signature of a submarine's engine. Scientists later gave this phenomenon a fittingly playful name, Fast Repetitive Tick, or simply, fish farts. The discovery was eventually honored with an Ig Nobel Prize, the annual award that celebrates real science that first makes people laugh, then makes them think. In the end, the great submarine scare that had unsettled Sweden for the better part of two decades turned out to have nothing to do with Cold War espionage at all. It was simply herring, doing what herring naturally do, deep beneath the waves. Fittingly, once the mystery was solved, reports of enemy submarines in Swedish waters dropped to zero, proving that sometimes the most advanced military technology in the world can still be outsmarted by something as small and ordinary as a fish.
