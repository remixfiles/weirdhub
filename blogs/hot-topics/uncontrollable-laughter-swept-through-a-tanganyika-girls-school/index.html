The Day Laughter Turned Into a Terrifying Outbreak in Africa

By WeirdHub Team • 20 September, 2026

How a boarding school room sparked an uncontrollable crisis

On a quiet Tuesday morning in January 1962, the air inside a mission boarding school for girls in Kashasha, a remote village in what is now Tanzania, was shattered by sudden giggling. What began as a seemingly harmless fit of laughter among three young students quickly turned into something far darker. Within hours, the giggles grew louder, wilder, and impossible to stop. Instead of bringing cheer or amusement, this laughter twisted into sheer distress, driving the girls into violent bouts of weeping, screaming, and relentless chest pain. Panic rippled through the hallways as classmates watching their friends collapse in agony suddenly found themselves laughing uncontrollably too. Out of the one hundred and fifty-nine students attending the mission academy, ninety-five became completely incapacitated by these terrifying fits, which would subside only to strike again without warning. Physical exhaustion set in rapidly, accompanied by dizziness, breathing difficulties, fainting spells, and skin rashes. School officials and terrified teachers tried everything to restore order and quiet the children, but reason had no power over whatever had seized the dormitories. Unable to stop the spreading chaos, the authorities were forced to shut the boarding gates on March 18 and send every student back home, hoping fresh air and familiar family routines would cure the mysterious affliction.

A silent contagion spreads across the hills

Sending the students away did not extinguish the fire, it only scattered the burning embers across the entire region. As the young girls returned to their small, tightly knit farming villages, the strange affliction jumped from home to home and village to village. Relatives and neighbors who stepped forward to comfort the weeping, laughing girls were soon struck by the very same uncontrollable spells. Within weeks, the quiet settlement of Nshamba was gripped by the outbreak, where more than two hundred people, mostly adolescents and young women, fell into identical episodes of intense hysterical crying and laughing fits that could linger anywhere from several hours to sixteen exhausting days. When officials attempted to reopen the Kashasha school in May, the nightmare rekindled immediately, forcing a second closure just weeks later. The contagion leaped boundaries, striking the Ramashenye girls middle school near Bukoba and silencing classrooms across the district. Over the course of eighteen grueling months, fourteen different schools were forced to shut down, and roughly one thousand people found their daily lives held hostage by an invisible force that left medical officers, police inspectors, and local leaders utterly baffled.

Unmasking the invisible trigger behind the terror

Medical officers and foreign doctors rushed to the affected communities to uncover the root cause, expecting to find a toxic plant, contaminated drinking water, or an aggressive new bacterial infection attacking the nervous system. Yet, despite rigorous blood screenings, environmental checks, and physical examinations, every lab test returned clean. The victims showed no underlying organic disease, no brain abnormalities, and miraculously, not a single person died throughout the entire year and a half of terror. Eventually, medical pioneers A. M. Rankin and P. J. Philip documented the bizarre epidemic in the Central African Journal of Medicine, diagnosing it as a massive outbreak of Mass Psychogenic Illness. The real culprit was neither viral nor comical, but profound psychological pressure. Tanganyika had gained national independence only months earlier in December 1961, ushering in widespread cultural instability, higher academic expectations, and strict new disciplinary codes in missionary schools. Caught between conservative domestic traditions and sudden modernization, the young students experienced overwhelming internal stress that they had no acceptable way to voice. Unable to rebel or retreat, their bodies converted that silent mental trauma into involuntary physical convulsions that mimicked laughter. Once authorities acknowledged the deep emotional strain and allowed communities time to rest and readjust without fear or superstition, the laughter gradually faded into silence, leaving behind one of the most chilling, misunderstood psychological mysteries in modern human history.
