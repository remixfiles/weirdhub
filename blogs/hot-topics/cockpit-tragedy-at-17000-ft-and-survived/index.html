The Pilot Who Was Sucked Out of a Cockpit at 17,000 Feet and Survived

By Mr Mannat • 16 September, 2026

A Sudden Nightmare in the Skies Above England

On a bright Sunday morning on June 10, 1990, British Airways Flight 5390 lifted off from Birmingham Airport in England, carrying eighty-one passengers and six crew members bound for the sunny coastal city of Malaga, Spain. Everything felt entirely routine and pleasant as the BAC One-Eleven twin-engine jet climbed steadily through the early clouds toward its scheduled cruising altitude. By the time the aircraft passed seventeen thousand feet over the serene Oxfordshire countryside, the flight deck crew loosened their shoulder harnesses, and cabin attendants began preparing the first round of morning tea and breakfast services. But at precisely thirteen minutes into the journey, this peaceful atmosphere was violently shattered by a sudden, deafening explosive bang. Under immense internal cabin pressure, the left-hand windscreen directly in front of the captain blew out of its metal frame with catastrophic force. In less than a second, explosive decompression tore through the cabin, ripping the flight deck door off its hinges and hurling loose navigation maps, flight manuals, and debris across the cockpit in a blinding fog of moisture. The escaping air instantly dragged forty-two-year-old Captain Tim Lancaster right out of his seat. He was sucked headfirst through the gaping window frame into the freezing sky. His upper torso slammed back over the cockpit roof while his ankles miraculously snagged around the flight control yoke, leaving him suspended outside the speeding jet at nearly four hundred miles per hour.

A Desperate Grip Between Life and Death

Inside the violent maelstrom of the flight deck, an unbelievable life-and-death struggle immediately unfolded before the eyes of the crew. Flight attendant Nigel Ogden, who was stepping into the cockpit to ask the pilots for their breakfast preferences, heard the explosion and instantly lunged forward across the center console. He managed to catch Lancaster by the ankles just seconds before the captain disappeared completely into the open air. However, the gale-force slipstream roaring through the cockpit was so punishing that Ogden himself began sliding headfirst out the window, his own face freezing in the hurricane-like winds. Hearing his frantic shouts, cabin crew member John Heward dashed into the chaos and threw his arms around Ogden’s waist to anchor him to the captain's chair, while flight attendant Simon Rogers strapped himself into a passenger jump seat and grabbed Lancaster's legs to reinforce the grip. Outside the cockpit, Lancaster was being battered mercilessly against the exterior fuselage in temperatures plunged below minus seventeen degrees Celsius. His face was pressed flat against the overhead glass, blood covered his chest, and his eyes remained wide open and completely unblinking. Inside, the terrified crew members genuinely believed their captain had already perished from lack of oxygen and the brutal cold. Even so, they steadfastly refused to let go of his legs, knowing that if his body flew backward into the tail-mounted engines or damaged the wing surfaces, the entire aircraft would plummet and everyone on board would die.

One Pilot Against the Odds and an Unbelievable Miracle

With his captain dangling limp outside the cockpit and the cockpit filled with deafening turbulence, thirty-nine-year-old co-pilot Alastair Atchison had to take instant, single-handed command of the stricken airliner. He deactivated the autopilot, fought the shuddering flight controls, and immediately pushed the nose of the plane down into a steep emergency dive toward safer air. The cockpit was so overwhelmed by wind noise and scattered paper that Atchison could barely hear air traffic control, but he managed to transmit an urgent Mayday call while descending to eleven thousand feet so passengers could breathe without oxygen masks. Navigating entirely by hand with extraordinary calm, Atchison diverted toward Southampton Airport along the southern coast of England. For nearly twenty agonizing minutes, the cabin crew held onto Lancaster’s frozen legs with raw physical determination, their fingers numb and muscles tearing under the immense wind drag. Against all imaginable odds, Atchison aligned the aircraft with the runway and made a picture-perfect emergency landing at 08:55. Paramedics and fire crews swarmed the plane, fully expecting to recover a deceased pilot from the windshield. To everyone's utter astonishment, Lancaster began showing faint signs of life as soon as the plane stopped. He had survived the extreme ordeal thanks to hypothermia and oxygen deprivation slowing his metabolism, escaping with a fractured elbow, a broken wrist, severe frostbite, and shock. Official investigators later found that eighty-four of the replacement windscreen bolts were slightly undersized, but the breathtaking courage of the crew and the remarkable skill of the co-pilot transformed a fatal disaster into the most incredible survival story in commercial aviation.
