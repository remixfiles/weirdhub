The Invisible Poison: How a Fake TV Prank Became a Real-Life Spy Assassination at a Busy International Airport

By WeirdHub Team • 15 November, 2026

A Routine Morning Departure Turns Into a Silent, Deadly Ambush

On the morning of February 13, 2017, Terminal 2 at Kuala Lumpur International Airport in Malaysia was filled with the usual noise of travelers rushing to catch their flights. Among the crowd was a 45-year-old man wearing a blue blazer and carrying a black backpack, standing calmly in front of an automated self-check-in kiosk. He was waiting to board a 10:50 AM AirAsia flight to Macau, where he had lived quietly in exile for years. His travel documents identified him as Kim Chol, a North Korean citizen traveling on a diplomatic passport, though his true identity was far more significant. He was Kim Jong Nam, the eldest half-brother of North Korea’s supreme leader, Kim Jong Un. Within seconds, two young women approached him from behind in a coordinated move that lasted less than three seconds. One woman distracted him while the other swiftly smeared an oily liquid across his face before both vanished into the busy terminal crowd. Airport security cameras captured the entire encounter, including the striking detail of one attacker wearing a white sweater with the letters LOL printed boldly across the front. Feeling an immediate burning sensation in his eyes and face, Kim Jong Nam walked calmly to the airport information desk to ask for help, telling staff that someone had grabbed him from behind and splashed liquid on his face. Within minutes, his condition deteriorated rapidly as he began sweating profusely, experiencing severe dizziness, and struggling to breathe. Airport medical personnel rushed him toward a nearby hospital, but the toxic substance had already overwhelmed his nervous system, and he died in the ambulance roughly fifteen to twenty minutes after the attack.

Unmasking the Deadly Nerve Agent and the Unwitting Assassins

When Malaysian forensic experts and medical examiners began investigating the sudden death, they uncovered a chilling reality that shocked intelligence agencies across the globe. Toxicology reports revealed that the oily liquid wiped onto Kim Jong Nam’s face was VX nerve agent, a synthetic chemical compound classified by the United Nations as a weapon of mass destruction. Just a tiny drop of VX absorbed through the skin is enough to disrupt the human nervous system, causing muscles to spasm uncontrollably and leading to fatal respiratory failure within minutes. In a bizarre twist, investigators searching Kim Jong Nam’s backpack found nearly one hundred thousand dollars in cash, four North Korean diplomatic passports under the alias Kim Chol, and twelve vials of atropine, an antidote specifically used to treat nerve agent poisoning, proving he had lived in constant fear of assassination. Soon after the attack, Malaysian police arrested two young women identified from the airport surveillance footage: Siti Aisyah, an Indonesian woman, and Doan Thi Huong, a Vietnamese national. During interrogation and subsequent court proceedings, an astonishing spy plot emerged that sounded more like a thriller movie than real life. Both women insisted they had no idea they were committing murder and believed they were participating in a harmless hidden-camera comedy prank for a Japanese television show. Evidence showed that weeks before the attack, handlers had recruited both women separately in Kuala Lumpur, paying them small sums of cash to practice smearing harmless baby oil, lotion, or hot sauce onto the faces of unsuspecting strangers in shopping malls and hotels. On the morning of February 13, their handlers instructed them to perform the exact same prank on Kim Jong Nam, handing them a liquid they assumed was ordinary prank oil, unaware that they were delivering one of the deadliest chemical weapons ever created.

Royal Exile, Global Fallout, and the Unanswered Questions of Justice

To understand why a man living quietly in Macau became the target of a state-sponsored chemical attack, investigators looked back at the turbulent history of North Korea’s ruling dynasty. Kim Jong Nam was once considered the natural heir to his father, former leader Kim Jong Il, having been educated in Switzerland and groomed for power. However, his fall from grace began in 2001 when he was caught trying to enter Japan using a fake Dominican Republic passport to visit Tokyo Disneyland, an embarrassing incident that permanently ruined his political standing in Pyongyang. Living in exile under Chinese protection in Macau and Beijing, Kim Jong Nam occasionally spoke to foreign journalists, openly criticizing his younger half-brother Kim Jong Un’s leadership and predicting that the regime would eventually collapse. Intelligence agencies from the United States and South Korea quickly concluded that Pyongyang ordered the assassination to eliminate a potential rival who could ever be used by foreign powers to replace the current ruler. The murder triggered an intense diplomatic crisis between Malaysia and North Korea, leading to expelled ambassadors, travel bans on citizens, and global condemnation over the use of a banned chemical weapon in a crowded civilian airport. Malaysian police identified four North Korean suspects who had orchestrated the entire operation, supplied the VX agent, and coached the two women, yet all four men boarded flights out of Kuala Lumpur on the very day of the murder and escaped safely back to Pyongyang. Left behind to face the death penalty in a Malaysian courtroom, Siti Aisyah and Doan Thi Huong endured more than two years of high-profile legal battles before prosecutors unexpectedly dropped the murder charges against Aisyah in March 2019, followed shortly by a reduced sentence for Huong that allowed her release in May 2019, leaving the true masterminds beyond the reach of international justice.
