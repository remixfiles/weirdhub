These Popsicles Look Delicious. They're Actually Made From Sewage.

By John Lee • 12 September, 2026

A Graduation Project That Turned Into a Global Sensation

In 2017, three visual communication design students from the National Taiwan University of the Arts set out to make people care about something most of us walk past every day: dirty water. Hung I-chen, Guo Yi-hui, and Cheng Yu-ti traveled across Taiwan, collecting water samples from one hundred different locations — rivers, harbors, storm drains, and wastewater outlets. This was no ordinary science project. It was their graduation thesis, and it would soon capture the attention of the entire world. Instead of writing a report or building a chart, the trio decided to freeze their samples into something instantly recognizable and universally loved: popsicles. The idea was simple but brilliant. A popsicle is one of the most comforting, joyful objects imaginable — bright, cold, and inviting. By pouring contaminated water into ice cream molds, the students created a jarring contrast between what looks appetizing and what is actually dangerous. Each of the hundred popsicles was later recreated in durable polyester resin, so the pieces could survive gallery lighting without melting away. Every popsicle came with its own custom-designed wrapper, listing exactly where the water had been collected. What began as a school assignment quickly became a full-blown art exhibition, first shown at the New Generation of Design Exhibition inside Taipei's World Trade Center in May 2017.

What's Really Inside These Popsicles

At first glance, the popsicles look like they belong in a trendy dessert shop. Some glow orange, others shimmer green or deep purple, colors that almost resemble mango, melon, or grape flavors. But none of that color comes from food dye. It comes from industrial pollution. Look closer, and the illusion falls apart completely. Frozen inside the "treats" are cigarette butts, tangled fishing nets, dead insects, oil slicks, and fragments of plastic packaging. According to the students themselves, roughly ninety percent of the debris they collected was plastic waste. The dirtiest sample of all reportedly came from Keelung Harbor, a busy port city on Taiwan's northern coast, which topped their unofficial ranking of the most polluted water source. The team wasn't just making art for art's sake. Their goal, as Hung I-chen explained to reporters, came from a simple observation: people rarely stop to think about the water around them. It's ignored, taken for granted, and treated as invisible until something forces us to look. By disguising pollution as dessert, the students made that invisible problem impossible to ignore, even for a few uncomfortable seconds.

A Beautiful Lie With a Powerful Truth

The project's real genius lies in that split second between attraction and disgust. Viewers see a popsicle, their brain says "treat," and then the details register: trash, sludge, and grime suspended in ice. That gap between expectation and reality is exactly why Polluted Water Popsicles spread so fast. Photos of the exhibit were shared across social media, picked up by major outlets including CNN, and reposted by design and environmental blogs from Asia to Europe to North America. The project was also nominated for Taiwan's Young Pin Design Award, a recognition of just how effective its message truly was. What makes this story stick with people isn't shock value alone. It's the craftsmanship behind the deception, the branded wrappers, the careful resin casting, the near-perfect mimicry of an artisanal dessert brand. It turns a serious environmental warning into something almost impossible to scroll past. Years later, the images still circulate online, still stop people mid-scroll, and still spark the same reaction the students hoped for: a mix of curiosity, discomfort, and a quiet reminder that the water flowing through our cities carries more than we ever see on the surface.
