He Spent 24 Years in Prison for a Crime He Never Committed — Then His Ex-Wife Came for the Money

By Mariyam Shignal • 7 September, 2026

The Wrongful Conviction That Stole a Life

In 1982, Steven Phillips was a young roofing contractor in Dallas, freshly married with a baby on the way. That same year, his world collapsed. He was accused of a string of sexual assaults across the city and, based largely on shaky eyewitness identifications, was convicted in two separate trials. To avoid a near-certain life sentence in a third trial, he pleaded guilty to additional charges. What followed was a nightmare that stretched across more than two decades. Phillips spent 24 years behind bars, insisting the entire time that he was innocent. His wife, Traci, stood by him for years, raising their son alone, visiting him in prison, and sending him money. But as the years dragged on with no end in sight, the marriage eventually broke apart, and the couple divorced in 1992, a full sixteen years before the truth would finally come out.

DNA Sets Him Free, but Justice Comes With a Price Tag

The breakthrough came through DNA testing, which eventually linked another man to the crimes. In 2008, Texas courts formally declared Phillips innocent, closing one of the most painful wrongful conviction cases in the state's history. Under a Texas law known as the Tim Cole Act, exonerees are compensated for every year stolen from them, roughly 160,000 dollars per year of wrongful imprisonment. For Phillips, that added up to a staggering package worth close to 6 million dollars, paid partly as a lump sum and partly as a monthly annuity. It was meant to be a form of justice, a way to hand back at least some of what the state had taken. But money like that rarely arrives without complications, and for Phillips, the real battle was only just beginning.

A Second Trial, This Time Over the Money

Two years after his release, Traci resurfaced, not with reconciliation in mind, but with a lawsuit. She argued that part of the compensation represented wages Phillips would have earned during their marriage, and since Texas treats such earnings as shared property, she believed she deserved a cut. In 2012, a Dallas County judge agreed, awarding her about 114,000 dollars. Phillips was stunned. He had already lost 24 years of his life, and now a court was ordering him to hand over part of the compensation meant to help him rebuild it. His legal team appealed, arguing the payout was never wages at all, but restitution for suffering behind bars. In 2014, the Texas Court of Appeals sided with him completely, ruling that nothing in the law allowed an ex-spouse to claim a share of an exoneree's compensation. Traci walked away with nothing from that fund, though she had separately received child support arrears years earlier. Reflecting on the endless legal battles that followed his release, Phillips summed it up bitterly, saying that whenever cheese appears on the table, the rats always come out too. It was a strange, almost poetic postscript to a story about stolen years, a fractured marriage, and the uneasy question of what justice is really worth once it finally arrives.
