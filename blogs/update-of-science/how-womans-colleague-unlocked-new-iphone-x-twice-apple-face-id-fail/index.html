One Face, Two Strangers: How a Woman’s Colleague Unlocked Her Brand-New iPhone X Twice and Left Apple Completely Speechless

By WH Team • 19 November, 2026

The Glitch That Should Have Been Impossible

In late 2017, Apple introduced the iPhone X to the world, promising a futuristic leap in mobile security that would make traditional fingerprint scanners look like relics of the past. The centerpiece of this technological revolution was Face ID, a sophisticated facial recognition system powered by a TrueDepth camera that projected over thirty thousand invisible dots onto a user's face to create a precise, three-dimensional mathematical map. Apple executives proudly stood on stage and assured millions of customers across North America, Europe, and Asia that the probability of a random stranger unlocking someone else's iPhone X using Face ID was roughly one in a million. For most buyers, this bold guarantee felt absolute, turning their smartphones into digital vaults accessible only to their own unique features. However, in December 2017, in the bustling eastern Chinese city of Nanjing in Jiangsu province, a woman known publicly as Ms. Yan discovered that even the most celebrated Silicon Valley engineering could be outsmarted by an ordinary coincidence. Ms. Yan had just purchased a brand-new iPhone X, excited to experience the sleek edge-to-edge display and the effortless convenience of unlocking her device simply by glancing at it. She carefully registered her own face during the initial setup process, confident that her personal photos, messages, and private applications were completely locked away from prying eyes. Everything seemed to work smoothly until a completely unremarkable moment at her workplace turned her confidence into pure disbelief. While sitting near a female colleague, Ms. Yan noticed that whenever her coworker picked up the phone or simply looked toward the screen, the device chimed open without hesitation. At first, Ms. Yan assumed she had accidentally left the phone unlocked or that the screen timeout setting was unusually long, so she locked the device manually and asked her colleague to look at it again. To her astonishment, the padlock icon on the screen clicked open instantly, granting the coworker full access to the phone as if she were its rightful owner. What made the situation even more perplexing was that the two women were not identical twins, nor did they share any family ties, blood relation, or distant ancestry. They were simply two unrelated coworkers who happened to work in the same office in Nanjing, yet the advanced sensors inside the iPhone X could not tell them apart. When Ms. Yan contacted Apple's official customer service hotline to report the bizarre security breach, the support representatives on the other end of the line refused to believe her story, treating the claim as an exaggeration or a misunderstanding of how the software functioned.

The Apple Store Demonstration That Shocked Everyone

Determined to prove that she was not imagining things, Ms. Yan decided that a phone call was not enough and resolved to demonstrate the flaw in person. She invited her female colleague to accompany her to a local Apple Store in Nanjing, carrying the newly purchased iPhone X with them like a piece of undeniable evidence. Inside the bright, crowded retail store, Ms. Yan approached the staff members and explained that her coworker could unlock her phone every single time without typing a passcode or using any clever tricks. Naturally, the store employees were skeptical, given Apple's strict internal assurances regarding the precision of the TrueDepth sensor array and neural engine. To settle the matter right there on the showroom floor, Ms. Yan locked her iPhone X, held it up, and asked her colleague to simply glance at the front camera. In front of the stunned Apple Store staff, the phone recognized the coworker's face immediately and unlocked the home screen without a single second of delay. Trying to rule out a simple software hiccup, the employees wiped the facial data, reset the Face ID settings, and walked through the registration process again, only to witness the exact same result moments later. Even more remarkably, when they reversed the experiment and registered the coworker's face as the primary owner of the phone, Ms. Yan was able to look at the screen and unlock it just as effortlessly. Confronted with live proof that their flagship security feature was failing right before their eyes, the store staff concluded that Ms. Yan must have been sold a defective unit with a malfunctioning TrueDepth camera. Apologizing for the inconvenience, the store managers immediately processed a full refund so she could replace the supposedly faulty device. Hoping the nightmare was over, Ms. Yan took the refunded money and bought a second, completely new iPhone X right there in the very same store, expecting the replacement to perform as advertised. She unboxed the fresh device, set up her Face ID profile once again, and turned to her colleague for one final test before leaving the building. To the utter shock of Ms. Yan, her coworker, and the watching store employees, the brand-new second iPhone X unlocked the exact same way when the colleague looked at it. It was no longer possible to blame a single broken camera sensor, because two separate factory-sealed devices had just failed the exact same real-world test within minutes of each other.

A Global Wake-Up Call for Facial Recognition Technology

Left with no technical explanation for why two different phones refused to distinguish between two unrelated women, the Nanjing Apple Store staff had little choice but to issue Ms. Yan a second full refund on the spot. Local media outlet Jiangsu Broadcasting Corporation soon aired a video report featuring Ms. Yan and her colleague demonstrating the phenomenon, and the story was quickly picked up by the South China Morning Post before spreading like wildfire across international newsrooms. Within days, technology writers, privacy advocates, and everyday smartphone users from New York to Berlin were debating what the Nanjing incident meant for the future of biometric security. While Apple did not publicly disclose whether Ms. Yan ever returned to buy a third iPhone, her experience sparked an intense global conversation about hidden biases and blind spots in artificial intelligence and facial recognition algorithms. Critics and computer vision researchers pointed out that facial recognition systems are only as reliable as the diversity of the datasets used to train them, and that subtle facial structures among East Asian populations had historically been underrepresented in Western tech development. For everyday consumers who had been encouraged to replace memorable passcodes with their own faces, the story served as a sobering reminder that convenience always carries a hidden trade-off. If a camera sensor could be fooled by an unrelated coworker simply standing nearby, users began to wonder how secure their banking apps, private conversations, and sensitive work files truly were when protected solely by biometric scans. At the same time, many readers found a strange sense of humor in the situation, marveling at the extraordinary odds of two near-doppelgangers ending up at the exact same workplace in Nanjing just as Apple launched its most ambitious camera system. Ultimately, the tale of Ms. Yan and her two refunded iPhones became one of the most memorable real-world stress tests in modern consumer tech history, proving that even a billion-dollar engineering breakthrough can be humbled by two ordinary faces that look just a little too much alike.
