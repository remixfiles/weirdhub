The Teacher Who Drew Microsoft Word on a Blackboard Without a Working Computer

By WeirdHub Team • 17 November, 2026

Turning a Lack of Technology Into a Lesson

In 2018, a Ghanaian teacher named Richard Appiah Akoto found himself facing a problem that could easily have stopped a computer lesson before it began: his school did not have a working computer available for the class. At Betenase Municipal Assembly Junior High School in Sekyedomase, Akoto taught information and communication technology, yet his students had very limited opportunities to work directly with computer software. Instead of allowing the lack of equipment to become the end of the lesson, he found another way to make the subject visible. Using colored chalk, he carefully reproduced the Microsoft Word interface on a classroom blackboard. The drawing showed the structure of the software and its different menus, toolbars, and other elements, giving students a visual representation of what they would encounter on a real computer. The approach was simple in principle, but demanding in practice. Recreating a software screen by hand meant paying close attention to many small details and arranging them clearly enough for students to understand. Akoto repeatedly invested time, patience, and concentration in this work because his goal was not simply to describe a computer program from a textbook. He wanted his students to understand how the software was organized. His classroom became a place where a familiar digital interface was translated into something the students could see on the wall in front of them. The story is compelling because the blackboard was not being used as a substitute for technology in some abstract sense; it became the medium through which technology itself could be explained. Akoto’s lesson demonstrated a practical form of teaching ingenuity: when the equipment needed for a subject is unavailable, a teacher can still create a meaningful visual model of the thing students are expected to learn. His Microsoft Word drawing eventually became far more than a classroom exercise. Photographs of the board were shared on social media, bringing international attention to the teacher and the unusual method he had used to teach computer skills without a working computer in the classroom. The lesson also gave students a concrete visual reference they could return to during class, making the unfamiliar language of computer software easier to discuss in a shared classroom setting. The board became a carefully constructed teaching surface, allowing Akoto to point to individual parts of the interface and connect them with the concepts he was explaining. In that setting, the drawing was not merely decoration; it was part of the lesson itself.

When a Blackboard Lesson Reached Microsoft

The photographs of Richard Appiah Akoto’s classroom drawing spread widely on social media, and the unusual story eventually reached Microsoft. What had started as a teacher’s practical response to a shortage of working equipment became an international education story. Microsoft invited Akoto to take part in Microsoft Education Exchange 2018 in Singapore, where educators from around the world gathered to share ideas and experiences. The invitation recognized not only the popularity of the photographs, but also the determination behind the lesson itself. Akoto had been teaching computer skills under difficult circumstances, using a blackboard to represent software that his students could not readily access on a working machine. His approach showed how a teacher could adapt a lesson around the resources actually available in the classroom while keeping the learning objective intact. The attention from Microsoft also turned the story toward a larger question: what happens when students are expected to learn digital skills but do not have reliable access to digital tools? In Akoto’s case, the answer had initially been to create a visual model by hand. His drawings included the Microsoft Word environment and other computer-related elements, helping students become familiar with the structure of technology even before they had regular access to it. The story also illustrated how a single classroom practice can travel beyond its original setting when people recognize the effort behind it. The images gave viewers a direct look at a teacher making the best of a difficult situation, and that visual simplicity helped the story resonate far beyond Ghana. Microsoft’s involvement did not end with the invitation to Singapore. The company also announced plans to support Akoto’s school through a local partner with devices and software assistance, while giving Akoto access to the Microsoft Certified Educator Program. In this way, the story moved from a remarkable classroom workaround toward practical educational support. The blackboard remained the symbol of the original challenge, but the international response began to change the material conditions surrounding the students. What made the episode memorable was not a claim that technology was unnecessary. Instead, it showed the extraordinary lengths to which a teacher was willing to go when technology was necessary but unavailable. The international attention also gave the episode a wider educational significance. It showed that a classroom story from Ghana could prompt discussion far beyond the school itself, while keeping the focus on a teacher’s effort to help students learn a digital subject despite limited equipment.

From Chalk Drawings to a Real Computer Lab

The most significant development came after the viral attention, when the promise of technological support became tangible for Akoto’s students. Microsoft later reported that the school received 27 new laptops along with a projector, screen, UPS equipment, a router, and one year of 3G connectivity. A computer lab was also created, giving students an opportunity to learn with actual technology rather than only through drawings on a blackboard. The change transformed the context of Akoto’s original lesson. The chalkboard method had allowed him to explain the visual structure of software when working equipment was not available, but a functioning computer environment could finally give students the hands-on experience that computer education requires. The sequence of events is what makes the story particularly powerful. First came the shortage of working equipment. Then came a teacher’s decision to recreate the software interface by hand. After photographs of that effort spread online, the story attracted international attention and reached Microsoft. Akoto was invited to Microsoft Education Exchange 2018 in Singapore, and support for his school followed. The result was not simply recognition for one teacher; it was an improvement in the learning environment available to his students. The 27 laptops, along with the other equipment and connectivity reported by Microsoft, meant that the classroom could move beyond a drawn representation of technology and toward direct engagement with it. Yet the original blackboard drawings remain important because they explain why the story captured people’s attention in the first place. They represented resourcefulness under pressure and showed that teaching can continue even when ideal conditions are absent. Akoto did not wait for perfect equipment before trying to make the lesson understandable. He created a visual bridge between the world his students needed to learn and the tools they could not yet use. Eventually, the bridge led to the real tools themselves. The story therefore stands as a reminder that access to technology matters, but so does the human effort behind education. A computer lab can provide hardware, software, and connectivity, but it still needs teachers who can turn those resources into learning. Richard Appiah Akoto’s classroom experience became widely known because a simple blackboard drawing revealed that principle in an unusually vivid way: when resources are limited, creativity can keep a lesson moving, and when that creativity is recognized, it can sometimes help open the door to better resources. The later arrival of equipment did not erase the value of the original method. Instead, it completed the story’s progression from explanation without machines to learning with machines, while preserving the teacher’s original example of persistence and practical problem-solving.
